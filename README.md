# FreshRoute

**Decision support for perishable produce distribution: what to dispatch first, where to send it, and when a discount pays.** Wild Bugs 24h Online Hackathon, problem statement PS 03.

Live app: https://wild-bugs-ten.vercel.app/ · Demo video: [docs/demo.mp4](https://github.com/Ryugi62/wild-bugs-freshroute/raw/main/docs/demo.mp4) · Spec: [SPEC.md](SPEC.md)

![mobile](docs/screenshot-mobile.png)

## What is different, and what is not
FreshRoute is a **one-day dispatch decision**: for the stock in the depot today, which batch ships first, to which market, and what to discount, each with a stated reason and **zero shipments that cannot physically happen** (a verifier re-checks stock, lane capacity, shelf life and delivery window). It does not forecast demand and it does not plan multiple days. Existing solutions are strong at what we skip:

| Type | Example | What it is built for | Source (vendor claims, not ours) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Retail AI ordering | Afresh | Multi-day demand forecast and ordering for store fresh departments; vendor claims 25% less waste, an independent WWF pilot reports 14.8% | [supermarketnews](https://www.supermarketnews.com/grocery-technology/ai-powered-food-waste-fighter-afresh-nabs-a-115m-investment), [pacificcoastcollaborative](https://pacificcoastcollaborative.org/?p=23621) |
| Dynamic markdown | Wasteless, RELEX | Expiry-based price changes in stores | [FAO agritech](https://agritechobservatory.review.fao.org/en/wasteless), [relex](https://www.relexsolutions.com/resources/case-nemlig-com/) |
| Indian B2B fresh supply chain | Ninjacart | Farm-to-retailer network with demand prediction (vendor claims 95-97% forecast accuracy) | [AIM interview](https://analyticsindiamag.com/how-ninjacart-utilises-ai-a-chat-with-ceo-thirukumaran-nagarajan/) |
| Open-source optimiser | Google OR-Tools min-cost flow | General solver; the same model class we use, no produce rules or explanations. We use our own 30-line min-cost-flow solver, not OR-Tools. | [OR-Tools docs](https://developers.google.com/optimization/flow/mincostflow) |

Our claim is narrow: a small, testable single-day dispatcher whose every line is explained and whose output is verified feasible. We do not claim better waste numbers than these products.

## The obvious fix vs ours
- Obvious: ship the nearest or highest-priced market first, hope it sells. A careful manager's version is a greedy rule: most perishable batch first, best margin market that still has demand and lane room. That rule is our strongest baseline, and we also give it a flat 30% surplus discount.
- FreshRoute: a max-profit min-cost flow over batch, market and lane. It never creates a shipment that would arrive with less than one day to sell or after the market's delivery window, orders dispatches most perishable first, and keeps a discount only where the simulation shows it adds net money.

## How it works (3 lines)
1. Every batch has remaining shelf life; a shipment is allowed only if shelf life minus travel minus handling leaves at least one sellable day and the shipment arrives within the market's delivery window.
2. The planner solves the allocation as min-cost flow (stock, demand, lane capacity), then searches discount levels per market and commodity against a shared cost model. A discount applies to every batch sent to that market for that commodity; the reason line lists the batch IDs.
3. A verifier re-checks stock, lane capacity, shelf-life and delivery-window limits, and a simulator scores the plan against four baselines on the same cost model.

## Results on 30 held-out seeds (1000-1029, never used while developing)
Run `npm run eval`. Full output: [eval-results.json](eval-results.json). **Every number table below is generated by that script into this README** (between the EVAL markers), so it cannot differ from the JSON. The datasets come from our own seeded generator, not from the organisers; everything is synthetic and shows behaviour, not real-world performance.

**Two dataset sets, shown side by side, same seeds, same cost model** (we did not pick the better one):
- *Default set*: the original generator. Shelf life is 35-100% of each commodity's maximum, four short-lived commodities and onions.
- *Shelf-life-binding set* (new in v3): `generateScenario(seed, { profile: 'shelf-life-binding' })`. 60% of batches are short-lived (life 20-45% of maximum, +0.5 d), the other 40% 50-100%; two random markets have a known transport delay of +0.4 to +0.9 d already in their travel time; demand is spread thinner (60-520 kg per market and commodity before capacity scaling, versus 150-900). Constants live in `SHELF_BINDING` in `src/adapters/generator.js`.

**Headline (most conservative over both sets and the three elasticities): net revenue +0.0% versus the strongest of four baselines, i.e. a tie with the careful rule-based dispatcher when demand barely reacts to price (shelf-life set, elasticity 0.5); +0.8% on the default set; never worse in 30 of 30 datasets; 0 hard-rule violations.** The gain grows to +8.6% (shelf-life set) and +4.1% (default set) only if demand reacts strongly to price (elasticity 1.5, the optimistic column; food elasticity in the literature is about 0.3 to 0.8).

**What the spoilage numbers say, plainly.** Against the two naive baselines (FIFO to nearest, FIFO to highest price) the plan avoids about 9,300-11,000 kg of really spoiled produce per dataset on the default set and about 13,700-14,200 kg on the shelf-life set, because they ship batches that cannot arrive sellable. Against the strongest baseline (an urgent-first, best-margin dispatcher that also checks shelf life) the plan spoils the same: 18 kg versus 18 kg on the default set, 1,223 kg versus 1,223 kg on the shelf-life set, almost all of it depot stock that no market can take inside its shelf life. We do not claim a spoilage reduction over a rule-based dispatcher; we claim the rule-based dispatcher's behaviour matched with a verifier and a discount search on top.

**Demand fulfilment (baseline values included).** Plan 73.6% versus 72.9% (strongest baseline) versus 44.8% / 33.2% (naive baselines) on the default set; 53.2% versus 53.2% versus 29.5% / 22.7% on the shelf-life set. The per-market spread (best minus worst market fulfilment) and unmet kg are in the tables. Fulfilment is a result of a profit-first objective, not a goal; the spread shows that small and far markets are left unserved.

**Carry-over definition (v3).** Stock that is not dispatched is split by remaining life: if life minus the holding day is below the minimum sell window it is *expired* (written off at 100% of procurement value, counted as really spoiled kg); the rest is *held* (holding cost plus the 50% carry-over write-off, counted as unsold but not spoiled). The same rule applies to every strategy.

**What a trip delay does.** In the trip-delay runs, the plain plan loses about as many kg as the rule-based dispatcher. A variant that plans with a +0.25 d safety buffer cuts really spoiled kg by 29% (3,261 to 2,328 kg per run, default set) and 11% (2,603 to 2,306 kg, shelf-life set) but earns less and fulfils less (the cost of the margin), so it is a trade-off, not a free win, and is not our headline plan.

**Why the stress-test nets (about 50-60k) are far below the normal-day nets (about 400k).** Each market accepts arrivals only inside a hard delivery window, so a trip only 0.25 days late (the average stress draw) turns whole shipments into spoilage: the decomposition table below shows a fixed plan falling from 446,898 to 162,499 INR from that one disturbance alone. **Why the plan wins only 41% of the stress draws at elasticity 1.0 (default set) although its mean is close:** both fixed plans fall off the same cliff, ties and small differences decide most draws, and at 1.0 the discount plan ships a little more into markets near their window limit (5,491 versus 5,319 kg really spoiled per bad day), so late trips cost it more; the shelf-life set is more extreme (negative mean nets for both). A tie counts as a win in "Plan beats baseline" when both plans are identical, which is why the shelf-life set shows 300 of 300 at 0.5.

The validator has no demand check on purpose: demand is a sales cap, not a hard rule (shipping more than a market buys is allowed and simply costs money as unsold stock), so only stock, lane capacity, shelf life and delivery window can be violated.

## Commercial flow for one warehouse (synthetic numbers)
Operator's morning, three steps: (1) load today's batches (kg, remaining shelf life) and each market's demand, lane capacity and price; (2) read the ordered dispatch list, most perishable first, each line with its reason, and load trucks in that order; (3) apply the discount lines, if any (only where the simulation says a discount adds money).
Monthly effect formula: **(plan net - comparison net, mean per synthetic day) x 26 operating days**. At elasticity 0.5 on the default set the plan beats the rule-based dispatcher by 404,755 - 401,552 = about INR 3,200 per day (about INR 83,000 per month); on the shelf-life set the difference is 0 (a tie). Against a dispatcher that ships FIFO to the nearest market without checking shelf life the difference is about INR 405,000 per day on the default set. These are synthetic days of 26 batches and 6 markets, not a field result; the same table is computed live in the app ("What it is worth to one warehouse").

## Limits (read before judging)
- Data is synthetic from our generator; no organiser dataset was available, so "hidden" means held-out seeds of our generator.
- Price elasticity is an assumption, not a measurement. The win shrinks to a tie at 0.5 on the shelf-life set; we show all three columns.
- Against a careful rule-based dispatcher the plan's edge is small (a tie to +0.8% at elasticity 0.5); its stronger claims are against naive dispatching, feasibility verification and explanations.
- Delivery windows are one latest-arrival time per market (days after dispatch), not time-of-day slots.
- Discounts are decided per market and commodity, not per batch, and only for one dispatch round (no discount timing).
- Not implemented: demand forecasting, multi-day simulation, map UI, real distributor or price feeds.
- Government post-harvest loss statistics are context only.
